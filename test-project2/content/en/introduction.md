Most documentation is decoration. A coat of paint over a website that could collapse if you leaned on it too hard — glossy screenshots, soft gradients, a hundred hidden layers between what you see and what's actually holding the thing up.

SLAB doesn't do that. What you see is load-bearing. No ornament standing in for structure, no drop shadow pretending to be depth. If a wall is here, it's here because it's holding something up.

## Why brutalism

Brutalism got its name from *béton brut* — raw concrete — and its reputation from refusing to hide the material doing the work. Poured concrete stayed poured concrete. Structural beams stayed visible. The building told you exactly how it stood up, because nothing was covering the answer.

Most web tools do the opposite: they bury the structure under so much decoration that you can't tell what's actually holding the page together, or move a single wall without the whole facade cracking.

{% callout type="note" title="The material is the message" %}
Nothing in this manual is here to look nice. It's here because it's load-bearing. If a section feels plain, that's not a missing feature — that's the point.
{% /callout %}

Here's how the rest of this manual is laid out.

### Raw materials

Markdown is the concrete here — unglamorous, honest, and strong enough to build anything on. No proprietary format hiding underneath it, nothing you can't read in plain text.

### Exposed structure

Every component — the sidebar, the header, the cards, the type — ships with its own visible seams. You can see exactly where one part ends and the next begins, and change any of it without touching the rest.

### No hidden foundation

Generate a static structure and set it on any lot you own. Nothing about it depends on standing on someone else's land.

### Built to code, not to trend

The rules here don't change because a trend did. Structure holds up regardless of what's fashionable this quarter.
